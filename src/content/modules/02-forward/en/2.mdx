Where does the forward of <Num of="m02.fwd" /> come from? The bank does not guess it. It asks itself: how can I get USD 1m in 3 months myself, without any exchange-rate risk? There are two routes.

- **Route A, the forward.** Buy the dollars today for future delivery. In 3 months you pay the agreed rate.
- **Route B, the money market.** Borrow francs today, use them to buy dollars straight away, and deposit the dollars until payment day.

Both routes are equally safe. On neither does the rate in 3 months matter any more. And both end the same way: with USD 1m on payment day and a fixed amount of francs paid for it. So they must also cost the same. Otherwise everybody would take the cheaper route and sell the dearer one (section 4). This idea is called **covered interest parity**. "Covered" means that on both routes the exchange rate is already fixed today.

### Route B step by step

The money market uses simple interest. For CHF and USD the convention is Act/360: 90 days count as 90/360 = <Num of="m02.t" /> of a year.

1. **How many dollars do you need today?** A USD deposit at <Num of="ex.rf" /> grows by a factor of <Num of="m02.growthUsd" /> in 90 days. For USD 1m on payment day, <Num of="m02.usdToday" /> today is enough.
2. **What do they cost?** At the spot rate of <Num of="ex.spot" /> that is <Num of="m02.chfLoan" />. You borrow this amount in francs.
3. **Time passes.** The deposit earns <Num of="m02.usdInterest" /> in interest and grows to USD 1m. At <Num of="ex.rd" />, the franc loan costs exactly <Num of="m02.chfInterest" /> in interest.
4. **On payment day** you have USD 1m and repay the loan: <Num of="m02.chfRepay" />.

So route B delivers USD 1m for <Num of="m02.chfRepay" />. Route A must cost the same. That gives the forward:

<Formula variant="accent"><i>F</i> = <Num of="m02.chfRepay" /> ÷ USD 1m = <Num of="m02.fwd" /></Formula>

### The formula

In general the franc amount grows at the rate of the quote currency (CHF), the dollar amount at the rate of the base currency (USD). The forward is spot times the ratio of the two interest factors:

<Formula variant="accent"><i>F</i> = <i>S</i> · (1 + <i>r</i><sub>CHF</sub> · <i>t</i>) / (1 + <i>r</i><sub>USD</sub> · <i>t</i>)</Formula>

In numbers: <Num of="ex.spot" /> · <Num of="m02.growthChf" /> / <Num of="m02.growthUsd" /> = <Num of="m02.fwd" />. If the USD rate is higher, the forward sits below spot. If the CHF rate is higher, above. Try it with the sliders.

### Money market or continuous?

The option models from module 04 onwards use continuous rates and <i>T</i> = days/365. There the formula reads:

<Formula><i>F</i> = <i>S</i> · e<sup>(<i>r</i><sub>d</sub> − <i>r</i><sub>f</sub>) · <i>T</i></sup></Formula>

Here <i>r</i><sub>d</sub> is the rate of the quote currency (CHF) and <i>r</i><sub>f</sub> the rate of the base currency (USD). With the example numbers this gives <Num of="ex.forward" />, which is <Num of="ex.fwdDiffPips" /> pips below the money-market forward. On USD 1m that is <Num of="m02.fwdDiffChf" />.

The difference is not an error. The same rate means simple interest in one case and continuous compounding in the other. Compounded continuously, money grows slightly faster, so the continuous formula discounts the dollar slightly more. All that matters is that you stick to one convention within a calculation.

<Derivation title="Derivation: two routes, one price">

Let <i>S</i> be spot in CHF per USD, <i>N</i> the USD amount at maturity, and <i>t</i><sub>CHF</sub> and <i>t</i><sub>USD</sub> the terms in years under each currency's day count. CHF and USD both count Act/360, so both are 90/360.

**Route B today.** You need <i>B</i><sub>0</sub> = <i>N</i> / (1 + <i>r</i><sub>USD</sub> · <i>t</i><sub>USD</sub>) dollars. They cost <i>Q</i><sub>0</sub> = <i>S</i> · <i>B</i><sub>0</sub> francs, which you borrow.

**Route B at maturity.** The deposit pays exactly <i>N</i>. The loan costs

<Formula><i>Q</i><sub>0</sub> · (1 + <i>r</i><sub>CHF</sub> · <i>t</i><sub>CHF</sub>) = <i>N</i> · <i>S</i> · (1 + <i>r</i><sub>CHF</sub> · <i>t</i><sub>CHF</sub>) / (1 + <i>r</i><sub>USD</sub> · <i>t</i><sub>USD</sub>)</Formula>

**Route A at maturity** costs <i>N</i> · <i>F</i>. Set the two equal and divide by <i>N</i>:

<Formula variant="accent"><i>F</i> = <i>S</i> · (1 + <i>r</i><sub>CHF</sub> · <i>t</i><sub>CHF</sub>) / (1 + <i>r</i><sub>USD</sub> · <i>t</i><sub>USD</sub>)</Formula>

**Continuous.** With continuous rates the interest factor 1 + <i>r</i> · <i>t</i> becomes e<sup><i>r</i> · <i>T</i></sup>, and the ratio of two exponentials is e<sup>(<i>r</i><sub>d</sub> − <i>r</i><sub>f</sub>) · <i>T</i></sup>.

**Where the <Num of="ex.fwdDiffPips" /> pips come from.** In the example <i>T</i> = <Num of="ex.T" /> in both worlds. The money market discounts the dollar by 1 / <Num of="m02.growthUsd" /> = <Num of="m02.dfMM" />, the continuous formula by e<sup>−<i>r</i><sub>f</sub> · <i>T</i></sup> = <Num of="m02.dfCont" />. A simple rate of <Num of="ex.rf" /> over 90 days corresponds to only <Num of="m02.rfCont" /> continuously. Plug in that value and both formulas give the same forward. The CHF rate would need the same conversion; at <Num of="ex.rd" /> nothing changes.

</Derivation>

<Desk title="Where the rates come from">

On the desk nobody works out the forward from policy rates. Forward points come straight from the FX swap market (module 03), where banks exchange currencies for a period. The rates in it are the rates at which a currency can actually be borrowed and deposited.

That is why the market forward often differs slightly from the formula with reference rates. The difference is called the **cross-currency basis**. It shows whether borrowing a currency through FX swaps costs more or less than borrowing it directly in the money market. Module 03 comes back to it. The logic of the two routes stays the same.

</Desk>

<Takeaway>

Two equally safe routes to the same destination cost the same. That is why the forward is not an estimate but spot times the CHF interest factor divided by the USD interest factor: <Num of="m02.fwd" />.

</Takeaway>
