The investor has francs in his account. In the example the CHF money market pays <Num of="ex.rd" />, the dollar <Num of="ex.rf" />. So why not simply buy dollars? Because extra interest is never free. Whoever wants more takes on a risk. Three routes show this, and they can be built from one another.

### Carry: earning the interest differential

The investor buys USD at spot and deposits them at the USD rate. In <Num of="m09.yMonths" /> months he earns an interest differential of <Num of="m09.carryChf" /> on USD 1m, that is <Num of="m09.carryPips" /> pips. At the end he changes the dollars back at the rate at expiry. If the dollar falls by more than these pips, the head start is gone.

The break-even is exactly the forward, <Num of="m09.carryFwd" />. That is no coincidence: the forward discount from [module 02](/en/module/02-forward/3/) is the interest differential. Economically, a carry trade is a bought forward. For the importer the same forward is a hedge; for the investor it is an open dollar position. The need makes the difference.

### Discount certificate: long USD plus short call

A discount certificate (generic) buys the dollar at a discount. In return the gain ends at a cap. In building blocks: long USD plus a sold USD call at the cap <Num of="m09.yStrike" />. The certificate costs <Num of="m09.certPrice" /> per dollar, that is <Num of="m09.certDiscount" /> less than spot. Two things sit in the discount: the USD interest you do not receive (with shares it would be the dividend) and the call premium of <Num of="m09.yCall" /> pips. The break-even is the price carried forward at the CHF rate, here <Num of="m09.yBe" />.

### Dual currency: the same curve, packaged differently

The dual currency investment from [module 08](/en/module/08-structured-products/4/) is a CHF deposit plus a sold USD put at the strike <Num of="m09.yStrike" />. For <Num of="m09.yMonths" /> months it pays <Num of="m09.dciYield" /> p.a. instead of <Num of="ex.rd" />. If the dollar falls below the strike, you receive USD instead of francs.

Because of put-call parity this is the same curve as the discount certificate: the same break-even, the same cap, the same risk. Only the packaging differs. Dual currency pays you the premium as interest; the certificate deducts it from the price.

<Callout variant="bad" title="When it goes wrong: the dollar falls">

With carry you lose every pip below the forward, with no floor. With dual currency and the discount certificate you receive dollars below the strike that are worth less than what you put in. The cushion only softens the loss. How quickly carry trades can turn was shown by the carry unwind whose stress peaked on <Fact id="m09-carry-peak" />: leveraged positions were unwound and amplified the market move. The [Riskometer](/en/riskometer/?pair=USDJPY&event=carry-2024) shows how volatility jumped.

</Callout>

<Callout variant="warn" title="Risk box: yield products">

- **Maximum loss:** as with holding dollars. For carry from the forward, for dual currency and the discount certificate from the lower break-even.
- **Leverage:** none. The exposure equals the amount invested.
- **Path dependence:** none. Only the rate at expiry counts.
- **Gain:** open for carry, capped for dual currency and the discount certificate. Generic examples: market variants differ.

</Callout>

<Derivation title="Derivation: carry, cap and no free lunch">

**1. Carry.** Money market Act/360, τ = days/360. Today you buy just enough dollars to have exactly USD 1m with interest at the end. Against a deposit of the same amount, at expiry you are left with:

<Formula><span>result = <i>N</i> · (<i>S</i><sub><i>T</i></sub> − <i>F</i>)</span><span><i>F</i> = <i>S</i> · (1 + <i>r</i><sub>CHF</sub> τ) / (1 + <i>r</i><sub>USD</sub> τ)</span></Formula>

**2. Discount certificate and dual currency.** With DF = 1/(1 + <i>r</i> τ) and put-call parity <i>C</i> − <i>P</i> = <i>S</i> · DF<sub>USD</sub> − <i>K</i> · DF<sub>CHF</sub>, ‘carry plus short call’ becomes exactly ‘deposit plus short put’:

<Formula variant="accent"><i>N</i> · (<i>S</i><sub><i>T</i></sub> − <i>F</i> + <i>C</i>/DF<sub>CHF</sub> − max(<i>S</i><sub><i>T</i></sub> − <i>K</i>, 0)) = <i>N</i> · (min(<i>S</i><sub><i>T</i></sub>, <i>K</i>) − <i>K</i> + <i>P</i>/DF<sub>CHF</sub>)</Formula>

The break-even is at <i>K</i> − <i>P</i>/DF<sub>CHF</sub> = <Num of="m09.yBe" />, the largest extra return at <i>N</i> · <i>P</i>/DF<sub>CHF</sub> = <Num of="m09.yMaxChf" />.

**3. No free lunch.** In the model E[<i>S</i><sub><i>T</i></sub>] = <i>F</i>. So the expected extra return of all three ideas over the deposit is zero. The extra interest is a premium for a risk, not a return for nothing.

**4. Time.** The cushion grows roughly with the tenor, <i>S</i> · (<i>r</i><sub>USD</sub> − <i>r</i><sub>CHF</sub>) · τ. A typical move grows only with its square root, <i>S</i> · σ · √<i>T</i>. After 1 month it is <Num of="m09.carry1" /> against <Num of="m09.sigma1" /> pips, after 12 months <Num of="m09.carry12" /> against <Num of="m09.sigma12" /> pips. The cushion catches up but stays smaller.

</Derivation>

<Desk title="Does the product suit you?">

This tool explains mechanics. It recommends no product and does not replace advice. Before taking out a product, a few honest questions help. Which need do I have: hedge, alpha or yield? Which option am I selling, and in which scenario does it become expensive? How much do I lose in francs if the rate runs far the wrong way, and can I bear that? Does the tenor fit my money? Anyone who cannot answer one of these questions does not yet understand the product well enough. That is no flaw, but a good reason to wait.

</Desk>

<Takeaway>

Extra interest only comes with risk. With carry you bear the currency risk from the forward. With dual currency and the discount certificate you sell an option: more cushion, but a capped gain.

</Takeaway>
