So far it has been about direction: will the dollar rise or fall? Now it is about size. How far will USD/CHF move by expiry, in whichever direction? Anyone with a view on that is not trading the rate but the **vol**.

Picture a trader who is waiting for an important interest rate decision. She has no idea which way the franc will jump. She only expects it to jump. A single call or put cannot express that view, but a combination can:

- **Straddle:** a long call and a long put with the same strike, usually ATM. It gains when the rate moves a lot, in either direction.
- **Strangle:** an out-of-the-money long put and long call, for example at the 25Δ strikes. Cheaper than the straddle, but it needs a bigger move.
- **Butterfly:** long a low call, short two calls in the middle, long a high call. It gains if the rate ends calmly near the middle. That is the opposite bet.
- **Seagull:** the importer's structure from section 1, a call spread financed by a short put. It is built for hedging, but it also takes a view on vol, whether he likes it or not.

<Derivation title="Derivation: what a straddle costs, and why">

**1.** At the forward, a call and a put cost the same (put-call parity with <i>K</i> = <i>F</i>). With Black-76, <i>d</i><sub>1</sub> = σ√<i>T</i>/2 and <i>d</i><sub>2</sub> = −σ√<i>T</i>/2 there:

<Formula><span><i>C</i> = <i>P</i> = <i>DF</i><sub>d</sub> · <i>F</i> · [<i>N</i>(σ√<i>T</i>/2) − <i>N</i>(−σ√<i>T</i>/2)]</span></Formula>

**2.** For small σ√<i>T</i> the bracket is about σ√<i>T</i> · φ(0) = σ√<i>T</i> / √(2π). The straddle has two legs:

<Formula variant="accent"><span>Straddle ≈ √(2/π) · σ√<i>T</i> · <i>F</i> · <i>DF</i><sub>d</sub></span></Formula>

**3.** In the example <i>DF</i><sub>d</sub> = <Num of="m06.discountRate" />, because the CHF rate is <Num of="ex.rd" />. So <Num of="m06.sqrt2pi" /> · <Num of="m06.sigmaSqrtT" /> · <Num of="ex.forward" digits={4} /> gives <Num of="m06.straddleApproxPips" /> pips. The exact value is <Num of="m06.straddlePips" /> pips.

**4.** What does this mean? If <i>F</i><sub>T</sub> is normally distributed around <i>F</i> with standard deviation σ√<i>T</i> · <i>F</i>, the expected absolute move is exactly √(2/π) · σ√<i>T</i> · <i>F</i>. So the straddle costs roughly the move the market expects. Buying it pays off if you expect larger swings than the implied vol prices in.

**5.** Vega is the derivative with respect to σ: in a straddle every leg counts, so it is doubled. In a butterfly the legs almost cancel, and the two calls sold in the middle have the largest vega. That is why it is short vega.

</Derivation>

<Desk title="Trading vol instead of direction">

On the desk a straddle is usually traded delta-hedged. What remains is a pure bet on vol: the buyer earns when the rate swings more than the implied vol says, and loses theta on every quiet day. That is the gamma-versus-theta contest from [module 05](/en/module/05-greeks/4/).

The butterfly has a second meaning too: as a quote, it shows how much dearer options far out of the money are than ATM options. That leads straight to the volatility smile.

</Desk>

<Takeaway>

Straddles and strangles buy movement: they are long vega and pay time value until the rate moves. Butterflies and seagulls sell movement: they are short vega and lose value when the market gets nervous.

</Takeaway>
