In the first section you saw what rate the importer ends up paying. Now we look at the same building blocks the way a trader sees them: as an option position with a payoff, a premium and risks. Three classics need only two legs.

A **call spread** is a long call with strike <i>K</i><sub>1</sub> and a short call with a higher strike <i>K</i><sub>2</sub>, both with the same notional. The payoff rises from <i>K</i><sub>1</sub> and stays flat from <i>K</i><sub>2</sub>. It never pays more than <i>K</i><sub>2</sub> − <i>K</i><sub>1</sub>. In return it is cheaper than the call alone, because the call you sell brings back part of the premium.

A **put spread** is the mirror image: a long put at <i>K</i><sub>1</sub> and a short put at a lower <i>K</i><sub>2</sub>. It suits someone who fears a falling dollar, for example an exporter who will receive USD in 3 months.

A **risk reversal** buys an option on one side and sells one on the other, both out of the money. For our importer that means a long USD call and a short USD put. The USD call protects him, and the USD put he sells finances the call. Depending on the strikes he pays a net premium, pays nothing or even receives money. Keep two cases apart:

- In a **25Δ risk reversal** the delta convention fixes both strikes: the 25Δ call and the 25Δ put. That is how it trades in the market. So its net premium is rarely exactly zero.
- In a **zero-cost collar** you choose one strike, here the call's. You solve for the other so that the net premium is zero.

The rest of this section works with the zero-cost collar.

### The importer's zero-cost collar

The importer buys the 25Δ USD call with strike <Num of="m06.rrCallK" />. It costs <Num of="m06.rrCallPips" /> pips, or <Num of="m06.rrCallChf" />. If he also sells the 25Δ USD put with strike <Num of="m06.rr25PutK" />, he has a 25Δ risk reversal. That put brings in more than the call costs. Net, the importer receives <Num of="m06.rr25NetPips" /> pips, or <Num of="m06.rr25NetChf" />. That is not zero cost.

For the zero-cost collar he keeps the call and solves for the put strike. A lower put brings in less premium. The USD put with strike <Num of="m06.rrPutK" digits={5} />, rounded <Num of="m06.rrPutK" />, brings in exactly what the call costs. It lies <Num of="m06.rrPutGapPips" /> pips below the 25Δ put. Together the structure costs nothing. At expiry:

- Above <Num of="m06.rrCallK" /> he exercises the call. At <Num of="m06.stUp" /> he pays <Num of="m06.upRrChf" />.
- Between the strikes he buys in the market. The forward of <Num of="ex.forward" digits={4} /> lies inside this band, which is <Num of="m06.rrBandPips" /> pips wide.
- Below <Num of="m06.rrPutK" /> the put is exercised and he has to buy at the strike. At <Num of="m06.stDown" /> he pays <Num of="m06.downRrChf" />.

Look at the Greeks in the payoff builder. Delta is <Num of="m06.rrDelta" />: the collar acts like half a forward. Vega is almost zero at <Num of="m06.rrVegaChf" />. The long call alone has <Num of="m06.callVegaChf" /> per vol point, and the short put takes away almost as much. So the collar hardly reacts to the overall level of vol. It does react to whether calls or puts become dearer. That is the subject of section 4.

<Derivation title="Derivation: the bounds of a spread and the zero-cost condition">

**1.** The payoff of the call spread per USD always lies between 0 and <i>K</i><sub>2</sub> − <i>K</i><sub>1</sub>:

<Formula><span>0 ≤ max(<i>S</i><sub>T</sub> − <i>K</i><sub>1</sub>, 0) − max(<i>S</i><sub>T</sub> − <i>K</i><sub>2</sub>, 0) ≤ <i>K</i><sub>2</sub> − <i>K</i><sub>1</sub></span></Formula>

**2.** Whatever always lies between two bounds at expiry must also lie between them today, otherwise there would be an arbitrage. With the discount factor <i>DF</i><sub>d</sub> = <i>e</i><sup>−<i>r</i><sub>d</sub><i>T</i></sup>:

<Formula variant="accent"><span>0 ≤ <i>C</i>(<i>K</i><sub>1</sub>) − <i>C</i>(<i>K</i><sub>2</sub>) ≤ <i>DF</i><sub>d</sub> · (<i>K</i><sub>2</sub> − <i>K</i><sub>1</sub>)</span></Formula>

In the example: <Num of="m06.csPips" /> pips lies between 0 and <Num of="m06.csWidthPips" /> pips (<i>DF</i><sub>d</sub> = <Num of="m06.discountRate" />).

**3.** For a long call and a short put the net premium is <i>C</i>(<i>K</i><sub>C</sub>) − <i>P</i>(<i>K</i><sub>P</sub>). In a 25Δ risk reversal the delta convention fixes both strikes. The net premium follows from them and is rarely zero: in the example you receive <Num of="m06.rr25NetPips" /> pips net. In a zero-cost collar you hold <i>K</i><sub>C</sub> fixed and require <i>P</i>(<i>K</i><sub>P</sub>) = <i>C</i>(<i>K</i><sub>C</sub>). Because the put rises with its strike, exactly one put strike satisfies this.

**4.** A limiting case links this to module 04. If both strikes equal <i>K</i>, a long call plus a short put is a forward at <i>K</i>. By put-call parity the net premium is then <i>DF</i><sub>d</sub> · (<i>F</i> − <i>K</i>), which is zero exactly at <i>K</i> = <i>F</i>. A collar therefore sits between two extremes: the forward (a band of zero) and the plain call (no put).

</Derivation>

<Desk title="The risk reversal as a market quote">

In the options market, "25Δ risk reversal" is not only a structure but also a price. It is quoted in vol: the implied vol of the 25Δ call minus the implied vol of the 25Δ put. Traders deal it delta-hedged. Its sign shows which side the market pays more for. More on this in section 4.

Companies usually know the structure as a collar or tunnel. With a premium or at zero cost, the put sold is an obligation, so a credit line is needed. Exporters use the mirror image: a long USD put and a short USD call.

</Desk>

<Takeaway>

A spread sells the protection you hardly need and makes the hedge cheaper. A risk reversal pays for the call with a put. In a 25Δ risk reversal both strikes are fixed, and the net premium is rarely zero. In a zero-cost collar one strike is solved so that it is zero: no premium, but in return a fixed band within which you buy.

</Takeaway>
