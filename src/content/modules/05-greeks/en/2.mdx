Delta tells you how much the call gains when USD/CHF rises a little. With a delta of 0.50 the option's value moves about half as much as the rate.

Gamma tells you how quickly that delta itself changes. Far out of the money delta is close to zero. Deep in the money the forward delta heads towards 1 and the spot delta towards <i>e</i><sup>−<i>r</i><sub>f</sub><i>T</i></sup> ≈ <Num of="m05.spotDeltaLimit" />. In between, delta changes fastest, and that is where gamma is largest. Across spot, the exact peak sits at <i>d</i><sub>1</sub> = −σ√<i>T</i> (derivation below). As a rule of thumb, gamma is largest when the forward is close to the strike, i.e. near ATMF.

For the importer this means: his call on <Num of="ex.notional" /> behaves today like a forward on roughly half the amount. If the dollar rises, that share grows; if it falls, it shrinks. Gamma measures exactly this adjustment. It is the reason an option can do more than half a forward.

<GreeksLabFigure mode="delta-gamma" caption="Drag the spot slider or the black dot itself: the black tangent at the top has slope delta, the tangent below has slope gamma." />

Try three things in the lab. Drag spot all the way to the right: the spot delta rises towards <i>e</i><sup>−<i>r</i><sub>f</sub><i>T</i></sup> ≈ <Num of="m05.spotDeltaLimit" />. That is exactly the spot delta of a forward: the call turns into a forward. The forward delta heads towards 1. Drag it to the left: delta falls towards zero. Then shorten the time to expiry to a few weeks: the delta curve becomes a steep step, and gamma shoots up.

Watch two details while you do. Both lead back to the forward. In the example, the steepest point of the delta curve is not at the strike line but further right, at spot <Num of="m05.gammaPeakSpot" />, close to today's spot of <Num of="ex.spot" />. At today's spot the forward sits at the strike, because the forward points are <Num of="ex.fwdPoints" /> pips. At the peak itself the forward is about <Num of="m05.gammaPeakFwdPips" /> pips below the strike; the derivation below shows why. And deep in the money the price curve even runs below the dashed payoff at expiry. The call works with the forward, not with spot. Measured against spot, its time value there is negative. That is neither a negative premium nor an arbitrage: the USD rate is above the CHF rate, so the forward sits below spot, and you cannot exercise a European option early ([module 04](/en/module/04-vanilla-options/3/)).

### Delta and gamma in the example

With <i>S</i> = <Num of="ex.spot" />, <i>K</i> = <Num of="ex.strike" />, σ = <Num of="ex.vol" />, <i>T</i> = <Num of="m05.T" />, <i>r</i><sub>d</sub> = <Num of="ex.rd" /> and <i>r</i><sub>f</sub> = <Num of="ex.rf" />, <i>d</i><sub>1</sub> is about <Num of="m05.d1" />. Delta is <i>e</i><sup>−<i>r</i><sub>f</sub><i>T</i></sup> · <i>N</i>(<i>d</i><sub>1</sub>) = <Num of="m05.dff" /> · <Num of="m05.nd1" /> ≈ <Num of="m05.delta" />. If USD/CHF rises by 10 pips, the call therefore gains about <Num of="m05.deltaPnl10Pips" />.

For gamma the density φ(<i>d</i><sub>1</sub>) = <Num of="m05.phiD1" /> comes into play:

<Formula variant="accent"><span>Γ = <Num of="m05.dff" /> · <Num of="m05.phiD1" /> / (<Num of="ex.spot" digits={1} /> · <Num of="m05.volFrac" /> · <Num of="m05.sqrtT" />) = <b><Num of="m05.gamma" /></b></span></Formula>

If USD/CHF rises by one big figure (0.0100), delta rises by about <Num of="m05.deltaChangeApprox" digits={2} sign={false} />: from <Num of="m05.delta" /> to roughly <Num of="m05.deltaUp" />. The lab reproduces the same value numerically.

<Callout title="Spot delta or forward delta?">

The lab shows the spot delta <Num of="m05.delta" />: per dollar of notional, the call behaves today like that many USD in spot. To hedge, you sell that amount. In the market, delta is often quoted as the forward delta <i>N</i>(<i>d</i><sub>1</sub>) = <Num of="m05.deltaFwd" />, the hedge with a forward to the same expiry. The difference is the factor <i>e</i><sup>−<i>r</i><sub>f</sub><i>T</i></sup>.

</Callout>

<Derivation title="Derivation in five steps">

**1.** Delta is the slope of the price curve, the first derivative of the price with respect to spot.

<Formula><span>Δ = ∂<i>V</i> / ∂<i>S</i></span></Formula>

**2.** Gamma is the slope of the delta curve. So gamma is the derivative of delta and the second derivative of the price.

<Formula><span>Γ = ∂Δ / ∂<i>S</i> = ∂²<i>V</i> / ∂<i>S</i>²</span></Formula>

**3.** In the Garman-Kohlhagen model the call's delta is Δ = <i>e</i><sup>−<i>r</i><sub>f</sub><i>T</i></sup> · <i>N</i>(<i>d</i><sub>1</sub>). Delta looks this simple because of the identity <i>F</i> · φ(<i>d</i><sub>1</sub>) = <i>K</i> · φ(<i>d</i><sub>2</sub>): when the price is differentiated, the terms coming from <i>d</i><sub>1</sub> and <i>d</i><sub>2</sub> cancel. For gamma you therefore only differentiate <i>N</i>(<i>d</i><sub>1</sub>) with respect to spot, by the chain rule. φ is the density of the standard normal distribution.

<Formula><span>∂<i>d</i><sub>1</sub> / ∂<i>S</i> = 1 / (<i>S</i> · σ · √<i>T</i>)</span><span>⇒ Γ = <i>e</i><sup>−<i>r</i><sub>f</sub><i>T</i></sup> · φ(<i>d</i><sub>1</sub>) / (<i>S</i> · σ · √<i>T</i>)</span></Formula>

**4.** Plugging in the example (<i>S</i> = <Num of="ex.spot" />, σ = <Num of="ex.vol" />, <i>T</i> = <Num of="m05.T" />, <i>r</i><sub>f</sub> = <Num of="ex.rf" />, <i>d</i><sub>1</sub> ≈ <Num of="m05.d1" />) gives Γ = <Num of="m05.gamma" />, the value from the worked example above. Gamma is measured in "delta per unit of spot": for every 0.01 move, delta changes by about <Num of="m05.deltaChangeApprox" digits={2} sign={false} />.

**5.** Where is gamma largest? Across spot, as in the lab, two parts depend on <i>S</i>: φ(<i>d</i><sub>1</sub>) in the numerator and <i>S</i> in the denominator. Set the derivative to zero. You need φ′(<i>x</i>) = −<i>x</i> · φ(<i>x</i>) and ∂<i>d</i><sub>1</sub>/∂<i>S</i> from step 3:

<Formula><span>∂Γ / ∂<i>S</i> = −(Γ / <i>S</i>) · [<i>d</i><sub>1</sub> / (σ√<i>T</i>) + 1] = 0</span><span>⇒ <i>d</i><sub>1</sub> = −σ√<i>T</i>, i.e. <i>F</i> = <i>K</i> · <i>e</i><sup>−1.5 σ²<i>T</i></sup></span></Formula>

So the peak sits slightly to the left of <i>d</i><sub>1</sub> = 0, because the factor 1/<i>S</i> pulls it towards lower rates. In the example it lies at spot <Num of="m05.gammaPeakSpot" /> with Γ = <Num of="m05.gammaPeak" />. At <i>d</i><sub>1</sub> = 0, i.e. at spot <Num of="m05.spotD1Zero" />, gamma is <Num of="m05.gammaD1Zero" />, and at today's spot <Num of="m05.gamma" />. In the example the differences are small. With longer expiries and higher vol, the gap between the peak and ATMF grows with σ²<i>T</i>. But the curve flattens at the same time, and gamma at ATMF stays close to its peak value. That is why the rule of thumb works: gamma is largest when the forward is close to the strike. Across strikes, by the way, only φ(<i>d</i><sub>1</sub>) depends on <i>K</i>; there the peak sits exactly at <i>d</i><sub>1</sub> = 0.

And because √<i>T</i> sits in the denominator, gamma near ATMF grows as expiry draws nearer.

</Derivation>

<Desk title="Gamma versus theta">

Whoever buys an option is long gamma. Delta-hedging it, they sell after rallies and buy after dips: movement earns money. In return they pay time value every day.

<Formula variant="dark"><span>P&L ≈ ½ · Γ · [(Δ<i>S</i>)² − σ²<i>S</i>² · Δ<i>t</i>]</span></Formula>

Break-even per day: σ · <i>S</i> / √365 = <Num of="ex.spot" /> · <Num of="ex.vol" /> / √365 ≈ <b><Num of="m05.breakEvenPips" /> pips</b>. If USD/CHF moves more than implied volatility leads you to expect for one day, the delta-hedged gamma buyer comes out ahead. The gamma share of theta is about <Num of="m05.thetaGammaDay" /> per day; the theta in the lab (<Num of="m05.thetaDay" />) also contains the rate carry of about <Num of="m05.thetaCarryDay" /> per day. Section 4 works through the details.

</Desk>

<Takeaway>

Delta is the slope, gamma the curvature. As a rule of thumb, gamma is largest when the forward is close to the strike. That is also where the gamma share of theta, the actual loss of time value, is largest.

</Takeaway>
