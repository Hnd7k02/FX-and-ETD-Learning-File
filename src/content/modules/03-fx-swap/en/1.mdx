Our importer has to pay USD 1m in 3 months. With the forward from module 02 he has locked in his rate. Now for a related question: what do you do if you do not need a currency for good, but only **for a while**?

Picture a Swiss company. It has francs in its account and has to pay a supplier USD 1m. If it buys the dollars at spot today, the money flows T+2 ([module 01](/en/module/01-spot/3/)): two business days later, under the holiday calendars of both currencies. So it pays the supplier on the spot value date, not today. One month later a customer in the US transfers exactly that amount back to it. So the company needs the dollars for one month only.

### Three ways to a month in dollars

- **Spot there, spot back.** It buys USD against CHF today and sells the customer's dollars again in a month. It does not know the rate for the way back today. That is open FX risk.
- **Borrow dollars.** It takes out a USD loan and repays it with the customer's money. That needs a credit line, and its francs sit idle in the meantime.
- **FX swap.** It buys USD 1m at spot today and, in the same deal, sells them forward for one month. Both rates are fixed today.

The third way is an **FX swap**: two FX deals in opposite directions for the same amount. The earlier one is the **near leg**, the later one the **far leg**. The company exchanges its francs for dollars for a while and gets them back afterwards. Here the near leg is a spot deal for value T+2. If the money has to flow today or tomorrow, you use a shorter near leg (value today or tomorrow), at a slightly different rate. The term of the swap counts from the value date of the near leg. The tool counts it in days, without a holiday calendar.

You already know the far leg: it is a forward from module 02. So an FX swap is a spot deal plus a forward in the opposite direction, done as a single trade.

### What the swap costs

In the near leg the company pays <Num of="m03.nearChf" /> and receives USD 1m at the spot rate of <Num of="ex.spot" />. In the far leg, after <Num of="m03.tripDays" /> days, it hands the dollars back at <Num of="m03.tripFar" /> and receives <Num of="m03.tripFarChf" />.

So it gets back <Num of="m03.tripCostChf" /> less than it paid. That is not an FX loss. It is the price of a month in dollars. The reason is the interest differential: dollars earn <Num of="ex.rf" />, francs <Num of="ex.rd" />. Whoever receives the higher-yielding currency in the swap gives the interest advantage back through the far-leg rate.

<Derivation title="Derivation: why the swap is a secured loan">

A swap on <i>N</i> USD with near rate <i>S</i> and far rate <i>F</i> has these cash flows from the company's point of view:

<Formula>
  <span>spot value date: +<i>N</i> USD and −<i>N</i> · <i>S</i> CHF</span>
  <span>after the term <i>t</i>: −<i>N</i> USD and +<i>N</i> · <i>F</i> CHF</span>
</Formula>

So for the period <i>t</i> the company holds dollars instead of francs. The far rate is fair if no other safe route is cheaper. That is interest rate parity from module 02, with simple money-market rates:

<Formula variant="accent">
  <i>F</i> = <i>S</i> · (1 + <i>r</i><sub>CHF</sub> · <i>t</i>) / (1 + <i>r</i><sub>USD</sub> · <i>t</i>)
</Formula>

It follows how many fewer CHF come back:

<Formula>
  <span><i>N</i> · (<i>S</i> − <i>F</i>) = <i>N</i> · <i>S</i> · (<i>r</i><sub>USD</sub> − <i>r</i><sub>CHF</sub>) · <i>t</i> / (1 + <i>r</i><sub>USD</sub> · <i>t</i>)</span>
  <span>with <i>r</i><sub>CHF</sub> = 0: = <i>N</i> · <i>r</i><sub>USD</sub> · <i>t</i> · <i>F</i></span>
</Formula>

That is the USD interest on <i>N</i>, converted at the far rate. The swap is therefore a USD loan secured by CHF; the interest sits in the rate. Because both rates are fixed today, the company carries no FX risk on the notional.

</Derivation>

<Desk title="Who needs FX swaps">

Banks use FX swaps every day to manage their liquidity in foreign currencies. If they are short of dollars and have francs to spare, they swap for a night or a week.

Companies use swaps when payments move in time. Investors holding securities in a foreign currency often hedge the FX risk with forwards and extend the hedge regularly with a swap. Section 3 shows how that works.

</Desk>

<Takeaway>

An FX swap exchanges currencies for a while: there at spot, back at a forward date, both rates fixed today. Its price is the interest differential, including the cross-currency basis in the market; no FX risk on the notional remains.

</Takeaway>
