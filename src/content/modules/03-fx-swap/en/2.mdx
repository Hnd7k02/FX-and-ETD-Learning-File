Now let us take a closer look. An FX swap consists of two deals in opposite directions:

- The **near leg** is the earlier deal, usually at spot for value T+2.
- The **far leg** is the later deal, at the forward rate for the end date.

What both legs share is the notional in the base currency, here USD 1m. The franc amounts differ because the rates differ.

The direction is named after the near leg in the base currency. **Buy/sell USD** means: you buy USD in the near leg and sell them in the far leg. That is what the company in section 1 did. **Sell/buy USD** is the mirror image: you hand over USD and get them back later.

<SwapLegsFigure caption="Top: the cash flows from your point of view; arrows pointing up are money you receive, arrows pointing down money you pay. Bottom: the forward curve. Drag the far point to another tenor or change the rates and watch the swap points." />

### The price is the swap points

A swap has two rates. But only their **difference** is traded: the **swap points**, far rate minus near rate, in pips. Whether the spot is a few pips higher or lower hardly matters. Both legs move together.

Where does the difference come from? The far rate is the fair forward from module 02. So the swap points are exactly the **forward points**. They reflect the interest differential between CHF and USD over the term. If the USD rate is higher, the far rate lies below spot: a discount. If the CHF rate is higher, a premium.

With **sell/buy USD** every arrow flips. You hand over the higher-yielding currency and are compensated for it: net <Num of="m03.mirrorNetChf" />. The swap points stay the same; only the direction changes.

The longer the tenor, the further the far rate moves away from spot. Over one week the swap points are <Num of="m03.points1w" /> pips, over one year <Num of="m03.points1y" /> pips. That is almost proportional to time, because the interest differential keeps running every day.

<Derivation title="Derivation: swap points from the interest differential">

With simple money-market rates and the same day count in both currencies, the far rate is

<Formula>
  <i>F</i> = <i>S</i> · (1 + <i>r</i><sub>CHF</sub> · <i>t</i>) / (1 + <i>r</i><sub>USD</sub> · <i>t</i>)
</Formula>

Subtract <i>S</i> and put everything over one denominator:

<Formula variant="accent">
  <i>F</i> − <i>S</i> = <i>S</i> · (<i>r</i><sub>CHF</sub> − <i>r</i><sub>USD</sub>) · <i>t</i> / (1 + <i>r</i><sub>USD</sub> · <i>t</i>)
</Formula>

The sign depends only on the interest differential. For short tenors the denominator is almost 1. Then the rule of thumb is

<Formula>
  <i>F</i> − <i>S</i> ≈ <i>S</i> · (<i>r</i><sub>CHF</sub> − <i>r</i><sub>USD</sub>) · <i>t</i>
</Formula>

In the example: spot <Num of="ex.spot" />, interest differential <Num of="m03.diff" />, <i>t</i> = <Num of="ex.mmDays" />/360. The rule of thumb gives <Num of="m03.approxPoints" /> pips; exactly, it is <Num of="ex.fwdPointsMM" /> pips. The denominator makes the difference.

Conversely, from spot, swap points and the USD rate you can back out the CHF rate. That is why swap points are also called the implied interest differential: whoever does a swap borrows one currency and lends the other.

</Derivation>

<Desk title="How swaps are traded">

On the desk, swaps are quoted in points, with a bid and an ask for the swap points. The spot for the near leg is only fixed as a reference rate. An outright forward is then simply a spot deal plus a swap. That is why the forward points of module 02 come straight from the swap market.

A swap dealer therefore has hardly any FX risk, but interest rate risk: if CHF or USD rates change, the swap points change. Very short swaps have their own names: overnight (today against tomorrow), tom/next (tomorrow against spot) and spot/next (spot against the day after).

</Desk>

<Takeaway>

An FX swap is two deals in opposite directions for the same amount. Its price is the swap points, and they equal the forward points: the interest differential over the term.

</Takeaway>
