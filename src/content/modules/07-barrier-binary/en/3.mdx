A digital pays all or nothing: a fixed amount if a condition is met. For the importer that is no hedge. The amount does not depend on how far the dollar rises. It is still worth a look: digitals are building blocks of many structures (module 08), and their price reveals which probability the market is pricing in.

All examples pay <Num of="m07.payout" /> and run for three months. The bet is always the same question about USD/CHF, just asked differently:

| Product | Pays if … | Price |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Digital call, strike <Num of="ex.strike" /> | USD/CHF is above the strike at expiry | <Num of="m07.digitalChf" /> |
| One-touch <Num of="m07.barrierUp" /> | the barrier is touched at any time | <Num of="m07.otChf" /> |
| No-touch <Num of="m07.barrierUp" /> | the barrier is never touched | <Num of="m07.ntChf" /> |
| Double no-touch <Num of="m07.dntLower" /> / <Num of="m07.dntUpper" /> | the rate always stays inside the corridor | <Num of="m07.dntChf" /> |

### Price equals discounted probability

The digital call costs <Num of="m07.digitalChf" />. That is no coincidence: the risk-neutral probability of ending above the strike is <i>N</i>(<i>d</i><sub>2</sub>) = <Num of="m07.digitalProb" />. The price is payout times probability, discounted at the CHF rate. With a CHF rate of <Num of="ex.rd" digits={0} /> there is no discounting.

The same holds for touch products. The one-touch at <Num of="m07.barrierUp" /> costs <Num of="m07.otChf" />, the no-touch <Num of="m07.ntChf" />. Together they make <Num of="m07.otNtSumChf" />: on every path exactly one of the two pays. Know one price and you know the other.

A rule of thumb from the desk: a one-touch costs about twice as much as a digital struck at the barrier. That is the reflection principle from section 1. In the example it is <Num of="m07.otVsDigitalH" /> times, because the forward lies below spot.

### At the hit or at expiry?

A one-touch can pay as soon as the barrier is touched or only at expiry. In the interbank market, payment at expiry is the usual convention. With a CHF rate of zero the two are worth the same. With a CHF rate of <Num of="m07.rdExample" />, the one-touch paid at the hit costs <Num of="m07.otHitRdChf" />, paid at expiry <Num of="m07.otExpRdChf" />. Money that arrives earlier is worth more.

### Double no-touch: a bet on calm

The double no-touch pays if USD/CHF stays within a corridor of <Num of="m07.dntWidth" /> around spot for three months. It costs <Num of="m07.dntChf" />. Whoever buys it is betting on a quiet market. That is why it depends so much on vol: if vol rises from <Num of="ex.vol" /> to <Num of="m07.volUp" />, the price falls to <Num of="m07.dntVolUpChf" />. If it drops to <Num of="m07.volDown" />, the price rises to <Num of="m07.dntVolDownChf" />. The buyer is short vega.

<Derivation title="Derivation: the digital as the limit of a call spread">

**1.** A call pays (<i>S</i><sub><i>T</i></sub> − <i>K</i>)<sup>+</sup>. Raise the strike by a small ε and it pays ε less in every scenario above the strike. So the derivative with respect to the strike measures the probability of ending above it:

<Formula>
  <span>∂<i>C</i> / ∂<i>K</i> = −<i>e</i><sup>−<i>r</i><sub>d</sub><i>T</i></sup> · P(<i>S</i><sub><i>T</i></sub> &gt; <i>K</i>) = −<i>e</i><sup>−<i>r</i><sub>d</sub><i>T</i></sup> · <i>N</i>(<i>d</i><sub>2</sub>)</span>
</Formula>

**2.** A digital paying 1 is therefore minus this derivative:

<Formula variant="accent">Digital = −∂<i>C</i> / ∂<i>K</i> = <i>e</i><sup>−<i>r</i><sub>d</sub><i>T</i></sup> · <i>N</i>(<i>d</i><sub>2</sub>)</Formula>

**3.** A call spread, long at <i>K</i> − ε/2 and short at <i>K</i> + ε/2, in a size of 1/ε, is the difference quotient of this derivative. Its payoff is a ramp from 0 to 1. With ε = <Num of="m07.spreadPips" /> pips it costs <Num of="m07.spreadChf" />, just <Num of="m07.spreadDiffChf" /> more than the digital.

**4.** For one-touch and no-touch, parity holds when both pay at expiry: one-touch + no-touch = payout · <i>e</i><sup>−<i>r</i><sub>d</sub><i>T</i></sup>. The touch probability comes from section 1. The double no-touch has no short formula: it is computed with an infinite series (Hui, 1996), which the tool truncates automatically once it is accurate enough.

</Derivation>

<Desk title="Digitals on the desk: the tight call spread">

Close to expiry and close to the strike, a digital is almost impossible to hedge. One pip decides between everything and nothing; delta becomes huge. That is why traders value and hedge digitals as a tight call spread. They choose the width slightly in their own favour: a safety margin the client pays for in the price.

The same applies to touch products at the barrier. Monitoring matters as well: the Monte Carlo check in the lab observes the double no-touch once a day. It is then worth more (<Num of="m07.dntMcChf" />) than under continuous monitoring, because no touch counts between two fixings.

</Desk>

<Takeaway>

Digital, one-touch and no-touch cost almost exactly the discounted probability that they pay. See the price and you see the probability the market is pricing in.

</Takeaway>
